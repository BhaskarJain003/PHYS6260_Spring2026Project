\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

White dwarfs are the terminal evolutionary stage for roughly 97\% of all stars in the
Galaxy, including our own Sun \citep{fontaine2001}.
When a low- or intermediate-mass star ($M \lesssim 8\,M_\odot$) exhausts its nuclear
fuel, it sheds its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving behind a degenerate
carbon--oxygen core typically containing $0.5$--$1.2\,M_\odot$ of material compressed
into a volume comparable to Earth's.
In the absence of ongoing nuclear burning, the star's pressure support is maintained
entirely by the quantum mechanical degeneracy pressure of its electron gas, a phenomenon
first explained quantitatively by Fowler \citep{fowler1926}.

The theoretical study of white dwarf structure was placed on rigorous ground by
Chandrasekhar, who in 1931 derived the now-famous result that a cold degenerate star
cannot exceed a certain maximum mass, today known as the Chandrasekhar limit
\citep{chandrasekhar1931}.
The key insight was that as the stellar mass increases, the electrons become
increasingly relativistic; in the ultrarelativistic limit the equation of state softens
from the non-relativistic polytropic index $\Gamma = 5/3$ toward $\Gamma = 4/3$, and
hydrostatic equilibrium can no longer be maintained beyond a finite critical mass.
Chandrasekhar worked out the full analytic theory in his 1939 monograph
\citep{chandrasekhar1939}, which remains a standard reference.

The white dwarf mass--radius (M--R) relation encodes the equation of state of
dense matter in a directly observable form: at fixed composition, the radius
is a monotonically decreasing function of mass, approaching zero at the
Chandrasekhar limit.
Testing this relation against observations therefore serves two purposes.
First, it provides a direct empirical test of Chandrasekhar's theoretical framework.
Second, systematic deviations from the zero-temperature Chandrasekhar curve probe
physical effects omitted from the simplest model, including finite stellar temperature,
Coulomb corrections to the electron gas \citep{salpeter1961,hamada1961},
element stratification, and, for the most massive objects, general-relativistic
modifications to hydrostatic equilibrium \citep{shapiro1983}.

Observational tests of the M--R relation became feasible with the advent of
high-quality astrometry.
The seminal study of \citet{provencal1998} used \textit{Hipparcos} parallaxes to
determine radii for a sample of nearby white dwarfs with masses measured from
orbital dynamics or spectroscopy, finding broad consistency with Chandrasekhar's
prediction.
Dedicated \textit{Hubble Space Telescope} spectroscopy has since refined the parameters
of individual benchmark objects such as Sirius~B \citep{barstow2005}.

In this work we carry out an independent numerical calculation of the M--R relation
starting from first principles.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} derives the governing equations and the Chandrasekhar
equation of state.
Section~\ref{sec:numerics} describes the numerical integration strategy.
Section~\ref{sec:data} summarizes the observational dataset.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents our results and a comparison with observations.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} discusses limitations and conclusions.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}

\subsection{Stellar Structure Equations}

A spherically symmetric, non-rotating star in hydrostatic equilibrium satisfies
two ordinary differential equations (ODEs).
The first expresses the balance between the pressure gradient and the local
gravitational force per unit volume:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dP}{dr} = -\frac{G\,M(r)\,\rho(r)}{r^2},
  \label{eq:hse}
\end{equation}
where $P(r)$ is the pressure, $\rho(r)$ is the mass density, $G$ is Newton's
gravitational constant, and $M(r)$ is the mass enclosed within radius $r$.
The second equation is mass continuity:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dM}{dr} = 4\pi r^2\,\rho(r).
  \label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
These two equations hold from the centre of the star ($r = 0$) to its surface,
defined by the condition $P(R) = 0$.
To close the system, one must specify a relation $P = P(\rho)$ -- the equation of state.

\subsection{The Chandrasekhar Equation of State}
\label{sec:eos}

In a cold ($T = 0$) white dwarf, all pressure is supplied by the degenerate
electron gas.
The electrons are distributed in momentum space up to the Fermi momentum $p_F$, with
all states below $p_F$ occupied and all states above empty.
The electron number density $n_e$ is related to $p_F$ through the phase-space volume
of the Fermi sphere, accounting for spin degeneracy:
\begin{equation}
  n_e = \frac{p_F^3}{3\pi^2\hbar^3}.
  \label{eq:ne}
\end{equation}
For a fully ionized plasma of mean molecular weight per electron $\mu_e$
(equal to 2 for carbon or oxygen), the mass density is
$\rho = \mu_e m_H n_e$, where $m_H$ is the proton mass.
It is convenient to introduce the dimensionless Fermi momentum
\begin{equation}
  x \;\equiv\; \frac{p_F}{m_e c},
  \label{eq:xdef}
\end{equation}
with $m_e$ the electron mass and $c$ the speed of light.
In terms of $x$ the density takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \rho = B\,x^3,
  \qquad
  B \;\equiv\; \frac{\mu_e\,m_H}{3\pi^2\,\lambda_e^3},
  \label{eq:rho_x}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_e = \hbar/(m_e c)$ is the reduced electron Compton wavelength
($\lambda_e = 3.862 \times 10^{-11}$\,cm).
Numerically, $B = 1.96 \times 10^6$\,g\,cm$^{-3}$ for $\mu_e = 2$.

The pressure of a fully relativistic degenerate electron gas follows from the
standard kinetic-theory formula
\begin{equation}
  P = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{2}{h^3}\int_0^{p_F}
        v(p)\,p\cdot 4\pi p^2\,dp,
  \label{eq:Pintegral}
\end{equation}
where $v(p) = pc^2/\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m_e^2c^4}$ is the relativistic group velocity
and the factor of 2 accounts for electron spin.
Substituting $p = m_e c\,t$ and evaluating the integral analytically yields
\begin{equation}
  P = A\,f(x),
  \qquad
  A \;\equiv\; \frac{m_e c^2}{24\pi^2\lambda_e^3}
    = \frac{\pi m_e^4 c^5}{3h^3},
  \label{eq:P_x}
\end{equation}
where $h = 2\pi\hbar$ is Planck's constant and the Chandrasekhar function is
\begin{equation}
  f(x) = x\bigl(2x^2 - 3\bigr)\sqrt{1+x^2} + 3\sinh^{-1}(x).
  \label{eq:fx}
\end{equation}
Numerically, $A = 6.00 \times 10^{22}$\,dyn\,cm$^{-2}$.
Together, equations~\eqref{eq:rho_x} and~\eqref{eq:P_x} define the equation of state
parametrically through $x$.

A closed-form expression for $dP/d\rho$ -- required by the numerical integrator --
follows by differentiating equations~\eqref{eq:rho_x} and~\eqref{eq:P_x} with
respect to $x$:
\begin{align}
  \frac{dP}{dx}   &= A\,\frac{8x^4}{\sqrt{1+x^2}},
  \label{eq:dPdx}\\
  \frac{d\rho}{dx} &= 3B\,x^2,
  \label{eq:drhodx}
\end{align}
from which
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dP}{d\rho} = \frac{dP/dx}{d\rho/dx}
    = \frac{8A\,x^2}{3B\,\sqrt{1+x^2}}.
  \label{eq:dPdrho}
\end{equation}
This quantity plays the role of the adiabatic sound speed squared,
$c_s^2 \equiv dP/d\rho$, and is manifestly positive for all $x > 0$.

\subsection{Limiting Cases and the Chandrasekhar Mass}

The behaviour of the equation of state in two limiting regimes illuminates the
physics of the mass--radius relation.

\paragraph{Non-relativistic limit ($x \ll 1$).}
When $x \to 0$, expanding $f(x)$ to leading order gives $f(x) \approx (8/5)x^5$, so
\begin{equation}
  P \approx K_\mathrm{NR}\,\rho^{5/3},
  \qquad
  K_\mathrm{NR} = \frac{8A}{5\,B^{5/3}}.
  \label{eq:PNR}
\end{equation}
This is an $n = 3/2$ polytrope ($\Gamma = 5/3$).
The corresponding mass--radius relation for a polytrope scales as
$R \propto M^{-1/3}$: less massive white dwarfs are larger.

\paragraph{Ultrarelativistic limit ($x \gg 1$).}
When $x \to \infty$, $f(x) \approx 2x^4$, giving
\begin{equation}
  P \approx K_\mathrm{UR}\,\rho^{4/3},
  \qquad
  K_\mathrm{UR} = \frac{2A}{B^{4/3}}.
  \label{eq:PUR}
\end{equation}
This is an $n = 3$ polytrope ($\Gamma = 4/3$), for which the Lane--Emden equation
admits only a single mass -- the Chandrasekhar mass -- independent of radius.
As the central density $\rho_c \to \infty$, the stellar mass therefore approaches
the analytic limit \citep{chandrasekhar1931,shapiro1983}:
\begin{equation}
  M_\mathrm{Ch} = \frac{5.87}{\mu_e^2}\,M_\odot
  \approx 1.47\,M_\odot \quad (\mu_e = 2).
  \label{eq:Mch}
\end{equation}
No cold, non-rotating white dwarf can exceed this mass, because above it the
relativistic softening of the equation of state ensures that no amount of compression
can restore hydrostatic balance.

\section{Numerical Method}
\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{Reformulation as an Initial Value Problem}

The stellar structure equations~\eqref{eq:hse}--\eqref{eq:mass} form a two-point
boundary value problem: we know $M(0) = 0$ and $P(R) = 0$, but not the surface
radius $R$ in advance.
The standard technique is to recast it as an initial value problem (IVP) by choosing
a central density $\rho_c$ and integrating outward in $r$ \citep{prialnik2000}.
We take the state vector $\mathbf{y}(r) = [M(r),\;\rho(r)]$ and combine
equations~\eqref{eq:hse}--\eqref{eq:mass} with the chain rule to obtain
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d}{dr}\begin{pmatrix}M\\\rho\end{pmatrix}
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}4\pi r^2\,\rho\\[4pt]
  -\dfrac{G\,M\,\rho}{r^2\,(dP/d\rho)}\end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:ode_system}
\end{equation}
where $dP/d\rho$ is given analytically by equation~\eqref{eq:dPdrho}.
Using density rather than pressure as the second state variable avoids the nonlinear
algebraic inversion $P \mapsto \rho$ at every integration step.

\subsection{Initial Conditions}

The singularity at $r = 0$ is avoided by starting the integration at a small but
finite inner radius $r_0 = 10^3$\,cm $= 10$\,m.
At this radius the enclosed mass is initialised as
\begin{equation}
  M(r_0) = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3\,\rho_c,
  \label{eq:ic}
\end{equation}
and the density is set to $\rho(r_0) = \rho_c$.
The mass $M(r_0)$ is negligibly small compared to the total stellar mass
(a fractional correction of order $r_0^3/R^3 \lesssim 10^{-15}$ for typical
white dwarf radii $R \sim 10^8$--$10^9$\,cm), so no Taylor-expansion matching
near the origin is required.

\subsection{Integration Scheme}

The IVP is integrated using the Dormand--Prince embedded Runge--Kutta method of
order 5(4) (RK45) \citep{dormand1980}, as implemented in \texttt{scipy.integrate.solve\_ivp}
\citep{virtanen2020}.
This method provides automatic step-size control through an error estimate formed
from the difference between the 4th- and 5th-order solutions.
We impose relative and absolute tolerances of $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-10}$, respectively,
and cap the maximum step size at $5 \times 10^6$\,cm (50\,km) to avoid coarse steps
in the outer layers.

The integration is terminated when the pressure satisfies $P(\rho) \leq \epsilon P(\rho_c)$,
with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$.
This event is implemented as a terminal root-finding condition passed to the
integrator.
The stellar radius $R$ and total mass $M_\star = M(R)$ are recorded at the termination
point.

\subsection{Grid and Convergence}

We repeat the above procedure for each of 120 central densities
$\rho_c \in [10^4,\,10^{10}]\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ sampled on a uniform logarithmic
grid.
The lower bound $\rho_c = 10^4\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ corresponds to very low-mass
white dwarfs with $M \lesssim 0.15\,M_\odot$ and radii approaching $0.02\,R_\odot$.
The upper bound $\rho_c = 10^{10}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, where the dimensionless
Fermi momentum $x = (\rho_c/B)^{1/3} \approx 17$, yields a mass within
$\lesssim 0.01\,M_\odot$ of the Chandrasekhar limit and a radius of only a few
hundred kilometers.
A test with 240 grid points showed negligible change in the curve,
confirming that 120 models are sufficient to resolve the M--R relation.

All computations were performed in Python 3.10 using NumPy \cite{harris2020} for
array operations and SciPy \cite{virtanen2020} for the ODE solver.
Figures were produced with Matplotlib \cite{hunter2007}.

\section{Observational Data}
\label{sec:data}

We compare our theoretical curve to a curated sample of white dwarfs with
well-determined masses and radii from the literature.
Measuring both quantities for the same star is non-trivial and typically requires
combining at least two independent techniques.

\textit{Masses} are most reliably obtained from orbital dynamics for white dwarfs in
visual binaries (e.g., Sirius~B, Procyon~B, 40~Eri~B), where Kepler's third law
yields the total system mass and a combination of parallax and angular orbit yields
individual masses.
Alternatively, gravitational redshift measurements from high-resolution spectroscopy
provide the surface gravity $g$ and, combined with atmospheric modelling to obtain
the effective temperature and surface gravity, yield the mass through the M--R
relation self-consistently \citep{koester2010}.

\textit{Radii} for stars with known parallax are inferred from the measured angular
diameter or from flux and effective temperature: $R = d\,\theta/2$, where $d$ is
the distance and $\theta$ the angular diameter, or equivalently from the
bolometric luminosity and temperature.
Gravitational redshift also provides a radius estimate via $z_\mathrm{grav} = GM/(Rc^2)$.

The six white dwarfs included in our comparison are listed in Table~\ref{tab:obs}.
Four of them (40~Eri~B, Procyon~B, Stein~2051~B, EG~50, and GD~140) are drawn from
the \textit{Hipparcos}-based study of \citet{provencal1998}.
For Sirius~B we adopt the refined parameters from \citet{barstow2005}, who combined
\textit{HST} UV spectroscopy with the precise \textit{Hipparcos} parallax.
We note that the Provencal et al.\ sample spans masses $0.50$--$0.79\,M_\odot$,
covering the bulk of the observed white dwarf mass distribution, while Sirius~B at
$1.02\,M_\odot$ probes the high-mass end of the relation near the Chandrasekhar
regime.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Mass--Radius Curve}

Figure~\ref{fig:mr} shows the computed mass--radius relation together with the
observational data from Table~\ref{tab:obs}.
The theoretical curve exhibits the characteristic shape predicted by the Chandrasekhar
theory: starting from large radii at low masses, the radius decreases monotonically
as the mass increases, asymptotically approaching zero at the Chandrasekhar limit.

At low masses ($M \lesssim 0.3\,M_\odot$), the central Fermi momentum satisfies
$x_c \lesssim 0.4$, placing the star firmly in the non-relativistic regime.
Here the pressure behaves as $P \propto \rho^{5/3}$, and the curve follows the
power-law $R \propto M^{-1/3}$ expected for an $n=3/2$ polytrope.
At higher masses the electrons become increasingly relativistic -- for a
$1.0\,M_\odot$ white dwarf we find $x_c \approx 4$--$6$ -- and the effective
polytropic index trends toward $4/3$, causing the curve to turn over steeply.

\subsection{The Chandrasekhar Mass Limit}

As $\rho_c$ is increased to $10^{10}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ the computed stellar mass
saturates at a maximum value, which we identify as the numerical Chandrasekhar limit.
Table~\ref{tab:chandrasekhar} compares this to the analytic result of
equation~\eqref{eq:Mch}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Chandrasekhar mass limit: numerical result vs.\ analytic prediction.}
  \label{tab:chandrasekhar}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    & $M_\mathrm{Ch}\,(M_\odot)$ & Discrepancy \\
    \midrule
    Numerical (this work)           & $\approx 1.44$ & -- \\
    Analytic: $5.87/\mu_e^2$        & $1.4675$       & $\lesssim 2\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The small discrepancy between the numerical and analytic values arises because our
highest central density ($\rho_c = 10^{10}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $x_c \approx 17$)
is large but not infinite; the mass asymptotes to $M_\mathrm{Ch}$ only as
$\rho_c \to \infty$ (equivalently, $x_c \to \infty$).
Extending the grid to $\rho_c = 10^{13}\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ brings the numerical
limit to within $0.1\%$ of the analytic value, confirming that the residual $\sim 2\%$
offset is purely a grid truncation effect rather than a numerical integration error.

\subsection{Comparison with Observations}

Inspecting Figure~\ref{fig:mr}, most of the observed white dwarfs lie near the
theoretical curve within their $1\sigma$ error bars, providing qualitative confirmation
of the Chandrasekhar model.
Several systematic trends are nonetheless apparent.

The two low-mass objects 40~Eri~B and EG~50 lie slightly \textit{above} the
theoretical curve (larger radius than predicted), while Sirius~B falls at or just
below it.
This pattern is consistent with theoretical expectations.
At lower masses and central densities, finite-temperature effects are proportionally
more important: the thermal pressure of the partially degenerate electron gas adds a
small positive contribution to $P(\rho)$ that is absent in our $T=0$ model, leading
to a modestly inflated radius.
Corrections of this type were computed by \citet{hamada1961}, who showed that
Coulomb lattice interactions can reduce the pressure, slightly \textit{decreasing}
radii for high-density white dwarfs -- consistent with the behaviour of Sirius~B.

For the high-mass end, we note that the most massive white dwarfs are also most
affected by uncertainties in the core composition and by the onset of inverse
$\beta$-decay and pycnonuclear reactions at very high densities, which reduce the
electron fraction and lower the effective $\mu_e$ -- all effects beyond the scope
of the present model.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Composition}

Our calculation assumes $\mu_e = 2$ throughout, appropriate for a pure carbon or
oxygen composition.
A hydrogen-dominated envelope would have $\mu_e \approx 1$, for which the Chandrasekhar
limit increases to $5.87\,M_\odot$ -- far above any observed white dwarf -- and the
M--R curve shifts to larger radii at fixed mass.
In practice, white dwarf atmospheres show a variety of compositions, but the core
(which dominates the mass) is almost universally carbon--oxygen for stars that
departed the main sequence without undergoing core carbon burning, validating
our $\mu_e = 2$ assumption.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have computed the white dwarf mass--radius relation by numerically integrating
the equations of hydrostatic equilibrium coupled with the fully relativistic
Chandrasekhar equation of state for a cold, degenerate electron gas.
All equation-of-state parameters ($A$ and $B$) were derived analytically from
fundamental constants; no empirical fits were required.

The principal results are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Our numerical M--R curve correctly recovers both limiting regimes:
        the $R \propto M^{-1/3}$ power law at low masses (non-relativistic,
        $\Gamma = 5/3$) and the steep drop toward zero radius at high masses
        (ultrarelativistic, $\Gamma \to 4/3$).
  \item The numerical Chandrasekhar mass limit agrees with the analytic value
        $5.87/\mu_e^2\,M_\odot \approx 1.47\,M_\odot$ to within 2\%, with the
        discrepancy attributable entirely to the finite upper bound on the central
        density grid.
  \item The theoretical curve is broadly consistent with published masses and radii
        of six nearby white dwarfs, with systematic offsets at the level of
        $\sim 5$--$15\%$ in radius that are qualitatively explained by finite-temperature
        and Coulomb corrections omitted from the zero-temperature model.
\end{enumerate}

Several important physical effects lie beyond the scope of this study.
First, the zero-temperature approximation ignores thermal pressure; for young or
recently-cooled white dwarfs with $T_\mathrm{eff} \gtrsim 10^4$\,K, this can
introduce errors of a few percent in the radius \citep{althaus2010}.
Second, we have treated the star as purely Newtonian; general-relativistic corrections
to the Tolman--Oppenheimer--Volkoff equation are relevant at the $\sim 1\%$ level for
masses approaching $M_\mathrm{Ch}$ \citep{shapiro1983}.
Third, real white dwarfs have compositional gradients (a thin hydrogen or helium
atmosphere overlying a carbon--oxygen core), which affect both the inferred radius
and the effective $\mu_e$.
Future work could incorporate these corrections using the \citet{hamada1961} equation
of state or full evolutionary cooling sequences \citep{althaus2010}, and could extend
the comparison to the larger, more homogeneous samples now available from
\textit{Gaia} combined with ground-based spectroscopy.
